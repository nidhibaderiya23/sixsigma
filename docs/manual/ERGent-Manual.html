Chapter 1Getting started

What ERGent is

ERGent is a dashboard for a city 911 ambulance (EMS) dispatch center. Here, the city is Ithaca, NY. It records the five times of every call: when the call arrived, when an ambulance was dispatched, when it left, when it reached the scene, and when it cleared. From those times it works out how long each part of a call took. It then shows a supervisor which zone is slow, which part of the call is slow, and since when. It flags zones that need attention, suggests a fix with its evidence, and later checks whether the fix worked. For the dispatcher on shift, it shows live clocks, so a late call is hard to miss.

ERGent only flags and suggests. It never dispatches an ambulance or changes staffing. People decide.

Why we built it this way

  • A monthly average hides the slow calls. So ERGent also shows the 90th and 95th percentiles and uses control charts.
  • Splitting each call into stages shows why a call was slow: dispatch, crew turnout, or travel.
  • A chart for each zone shows where the problem is, and since when.
  • A before and after check shows whether a fix really worked.

Who it is for

Role When they use it What they decide
Supervisor (the main user) At the start of each shift, and whenever a flag appears Which zone needs attention, which stage causes it, which fix to try, and later whether the fix worked
Dispatcher (the second user) In real time, during a shift Which open call is running late right now

How to start it

You need Python 3.12. The first time only, make a virtual environment and install the packages. Run these in the project folder:

python3.12 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -r app/requirements.txt

Then start the app with one command:

cd app && ./testme.sh

Open http://localhost:8000 in your browser. Stop the app with Ctrl+C in the terminal.

Live version: LIVE URL: (added after deploy). If your team has deployed ERGent, open that address instead. You do not need to log in.

Internet needed. The Ithaca map and its tiles load from the internet. If the map area is blank, check your connection.

Choose a role

The start page has two buttons:

  • I'm a dispatcher opens the live board and the History tab. Read chapter 2.
  • I'm a supervisor opens the Charts and Actions tabs. Read chapter 3.

You can change role at any time. Click Switch role in the top right corner. Each browser tab keeps its own board, so two people can try ERGent at once without affecting each other.

The Manual link in the header opens this manual in a new tab, from any page.

Shortcut links. Add ?role=dispatcher or ?role=supervisor to the app's address to open a view directly. Add &tab=History or &tab=Actions to open that tab, and &sim=1 to start the shift replay. For example: http://localhost:8000/?role=dispatcher&sim=1.

The data is synthetic

All the data in ERGent is invented. It was made by a script with a fixed random seed, so it is the same every time. It is shaped like real field data, but it contains no real patients, addresses or names. Nothing here describes real Ithaca emergency calls.

There are three data sets in the Dataset menu (the supervisor view):

Data set What it is for
Demo (42 days) Has planted problems, so the tool has something to find. Zone D's crew turnout gets slow from Sep 15 and a fix is logged on Sep 29. Zone C slows in the weekday rush hour from Sep 20.
Baseline (28 days) A stable city. A quiet dashboard is the right answer.
Messy file (3 days) Has planted data errors: missing times, out-of-order times, a duplicate call ID. ERGent lists them and leaves them out.

The dispatcher's Simulate a shift button replays one day of the demo data.

The six zones

ERGent splits Ithaca into six zones. The outlines on the map are illustrative. They are not the official EMS districts.

Zone Name
A West Hill
B Northside & Fall Creek
C Cornell & Collegetown
D West End & Southwest
E Downtown & South Hill
F East Hill & Belle Sherman

On the map, A, B and C run west to east along the north side. D, E and F run west to east along the south side. Zones that touch each other are neighbors. ERGent uses that when it suggests which zone should cover for another.

What the screens look like

The colors and layout can change between versions of ERGent. This manual describes screens by their titles and controls, so it still works if the look changes.

Targets used everywhere

Each call must meet these targets in 90% of calls:

Part of the call Priority 1 Priority 2 Priority 3
Dispatch (call received to dispatched) 64 s 64 s 64 s
Turnout (dispatched to en route) 60 s 60 s 60 s
Travel (en route to on scene) 8:00 14:00 19:00
Response (dispatched to on scene) 9 min 15 min 20 min

The priority 1 numbers come from NFPA 1710, a US fire and EMS standard. The priority 2 and 3 targets are our own assumptions, because the standard does not set them. A supervisor can change every target (see chapter 3).

Chapter 2Dispatcher guide

This chapter is for the dispatcher on shift. It walks through every part of the dispatcher screen, in the same order as the numbers on the screen. Click I'm a dispatcher on the start page to begin.

The screen at a glance

  • The header holds the simulation controls (Day, Speed, Start, Simulate a shift, Reset), a chip that shows the clock mode (such as LIVE 1x or SIM 120x), and Switch role.
  • Two tabs sit under the header: Live board and History.
  • Live board is for the calls happening right now. History looks back over the current shift and the one before it.

How to read a clock

Every open call has a running clock. Each clock has a target:

Clock Target
Waiting (call received to dispatched) 1:04
Turnout (dispatched to en route) 1:00
Travel (en route to on scene) 8:00 for priority 1, 14:00 for priority 2, 19:00 for priority 3
Total (dispatched to on scene) 9:00 for priority 1, 15:00 for priority 2, 20:00 for priority 3

A clock gives you a warning at 80% of its target. It changes again when it is past the target. Past the target means the call is late in that stage. A supervisor can change the targets (chapter 3).

The top row: counts

Five counters show the workload: Waiting, Dispatched, En route, On scene and Units available. They update by themselves.

1. Log a call

Use this card to enter a call by hand. In a real center, the call-taker's system would do this for you.

  1. Choose the Zone. The zone letters match the Ithaca map (A West Hill, B Northside & Fall Creek, C Cornell & Collegetown, D West End & Southwest, E Downtown & South Hill, F East Hill & Belle Sherman).
  2. Choose the Priority: 1: Life-threatening, 2: Urgent or 3: Non-urgent.
  3. Optionally choose the Clock speed (see "Clock speed" below). Leave it at 1x for real time.
  4. Click Add call.

The call gets an ID like LIVE-20261016-001. Its received time is stamped from the board clock. You do not type any time.

2. Incoming calls

New calls wait here until you dispatch an ambulance. Each row shows the call ID, the zone, the priority, and a Waiting clock.

  1. Look at the unit menu on the row. It lists only available ambulances. Ambulances from the call's own zone come first. Ambulances from other zones are marked with their home zone.
  2. Pick the ambulance you want.
  3. Click Dispatch.

If no ambulance is free, the row says No unit free. Wait for one to clear, or bring one back into service (see "Ambulances").

If the call has waited longer than its 1:04 target, ERGent asks for a delay reason first (see "The delay-reason dialog").

3. Dispatched

The call is assigned and the crew must get moving. The row shows the unit and a Turnout clock. When the crew leaves, click En route. If turnout ran past 1:00, ERGent asks why.

4. En route

The ambulance is on the road. The row shows two clocks: Travel and Total. When the crew arrives, click On scene. If travel ran past its target, or the total response ran past its target, ERGent asks why.

If the ambulance comes from another zone, the row says so, for example "(from zone A)".

Ambulances

This card shows all 12 ambulances in four columns. Each column has a count:

  • Available: free to be dispatched.
  • Responding: dispatched or en route. The chip says which.
  • On scene: at the scene, not yet cleared.
  • Out of service: not usable.

Each ambulance shows its ID and home zone, and one button.

  • Out of service takes an ambulance out of use, for example after a breakdown. If it was rolling to a call you logged, that call is flagged Breakdown. ERGent records "ambulance breakdown" as the reason when the call reaches the scene, so you do not have to.
  • Back in service returns an ambulance to the Available column.

Zone map

The map shows Ithaca with the six zones. The outlines are illustrative, not official EMS districts.

  • Hover over a zone to see its name, its status, how many calls are open there and how many ambulances are available.
  • Click a zone to pick it in Log a call.
  • The status comes from the clocks of that zone's open calls. A zone shows On track when all its clocks are comfortable, Watch when a clock is above 80% of its target, and Past target when a clock is late.
  • The map needs an internet connection to draw.

5. Resolved

A call moves here when it reaches the scene. "On scene" counts as resolved. The list shows the latest 60 calls and scrolls.

Each row shows the unit, the time, the delay reason (if any), and the final response time. The response time is marked as late when it passed its target.

  • A call that you logged by hand, and that is still at the scene, has a Clear button. Click it, then confirm "Clear call ...?". The row changes to Cleared and the ambulance becomes available.
  • Replayed calls (from Simulate a shift) show At the scene and Replay. They are read-only.

The delay-reason dialog

When you press a button that ends a stage that ran past its target, ERGent will not accept the press until you say why. A dialog titled Why was this late? opens. It tells you how long the stage took and what the target was.

  1. Choose one reason from the list:
    • Traffic or road closure
    • Ambulance breakdown
    • Unit from another zone
    • Unclear address
    • Weather
    • Crew not ready
  2. Click Save reason and continue.

The time you pressed the button is kept, not the time you chose the reason. If you click Cancel, the dialog closes and the press is not recorded.

The reason is saved with the call. The supervisor's suggested actions use these reasons.

The rules that protect you (mistake-proofing)

ERGent is built so that wrong entries are hard to make:

  • Only the next valid button works: Dispatch, then En route, then On scene, then Clear. Any other press is refused with a message.
  • You can only dispatch an available ambulance.
  • A late stage needs a reason. The reason is one of six fixed codes, so it can be counted.
  • Clear asks for confirmation first.
  • Times come from the clock. You never type a time.

Clock speed

The Clock speed menu on the 1 Log a call card sets how fast the board clock runs when no simulation is running. The choices are 1x, 10x, 30x and 60x.

  • At 1x the board clock is real time.
  • At 60x, one real second is one minute on the board. This is good for practice, because clocks turn late in seconds.
  • Changing the speed continues from the current board time. The clock never jumps.
  • At a high speed the board clock runs ahead of real time. The shift label in History follows the board clock.

The header chip shows the speed, for example LIVE 60x.

Simulate a shift

The header controls replay one day of the demo data on a fast clock. This is the best way to learn ERGent.

  1. Choose the Day (the default is Sep 20).
  2. Choose the Speed: 30x, 60x or 120x.
  3. Choose where to Start: 00:00, 06:00, 14:00 or 22:00.
  4. Click Simulate a shift.

The chip changes to SIM 120x (or the speed you chose). Calls appear and move through cards 2, 3, 4 and 5 at the times in the data. The button becomes Pause. Then it becomes Resume, and Run again when the day ends.

Good to know:

  • Replayed calls are read-only. You can still add your own calls, and they use the same clock.
  • Calls that were already cleared before the start time are left out.
  • You can change the Speed while it runs.
  • To change the day or the start time, click Reset first.

Reset

Reset clears everything back to zero:

  • All replayed and hand-logged calls disappear.
  • Waiting, Dispatched, En route and On scene all show 0.
  • All 12 ambulances are Available again.
  • Resolved is empty.
  • The clock goes back to real time, at the clock speed you last chose.

Download calls

Download calls (CSV) saves this session's calls as a spreadsheet file. The columns are the same as the data files in the project, so you can open it in Excel or load it back into ERGent's tools.

History tab

History looks back over the current shift and the one before it. A shift is eight hours: day 06:00 to 14:00, evening 14:00 to 22:00, night 22:00 to 06:00. History follows the board clock, so during a simulation it follows the simulated time. It refreshes about every 5 seconds while you look at it.

From top to bottom:

  1. The shift label. For example: "Current: day shift 06:00-14:00, Sep 16 · Previous: night shift 22:00-06:00, Sep 15".
  2. Four numbers: Calls, Mean (min), Standard deviation (min) and On time. They cover all priorities and only calls that have reached the scene. "On time" means within each call's own target. The mean is the average response time. The standard deviation shows how spread out the times are.
  3. Response time by zone (the chart). Use the Priority menu to switch between Priority 1: Life-threatening, Priority 2: Urgent and Priority 3: Non-urgent. Each zone has a box that shows where most of its calls fall, and a diamond for its average. A line shows the city's center, short dashed lines show each zone's limits, and a line shows the target.
    • A red diamond is a zone that is slower than the whole city. Hover over it to see why. For example: "Zone D is slower than the city: 9.3 min typical vs. 6.3 min. Main cause: crew turnout (100% of minutes over target). Top reason: crew not ready (2 of 2 late calls)."
    • A zone marked faster than the city is shown as "check the data". Be careful: very fast times can be a data mistake.
    • Under the chart, a note names the slower zones, or says "No zone is slower than the city as a whole."
    • The number under each zone letter (such as n=5) is how many calls it has. A zone with few calls has wide limits, so be careful with it.
  4. Suggested actions (on the right). ERGent's live tips for you. See below.

Right after you start ERGent in real time, History is empty, because the demo data is from the past. It fills up when you log calls or run Simulate a shift.

Suggested actions (tips T1 to T6)

Each tip has a short name, a zone tag, a plain sentence, and the evidence in small type. A note reminds you: "For the dispatcher's judgment; the tool decides nothing on its own."

Tip When it appears What it suggests
T1 Dispatch now A call on the board has waited longer than 64 s for dispatch Dispatch it now. It names the nearest free ambulance: one from the call's own zone first, then one from a neighboring zone. If none is free, it says so.
T2 Rebalance A zone has no free ambulance and a neighboring zone has two or more Move one ambulance to stand by in the empty zone.
T3 Cover An ambulance is out of service Ask the neighboring zones to cover that zone's calls.
T4 Route A zone's typical travel time is at least 1.25 times the city's (needs 3 or more calls in the two shifts) Check the route. If two or more late calls share a reason, the tip names it: reroute around traffic or road works, or keep an ambulance posted in that zone.
T5 Station alert A zone's typical crew turnout is over 60 s (needs 3 or more calls) Alert that zone's station: crews are slow to get rolling.
T6 Dispatcher load Fewer than 9 in 10 calls were dispatched within 64 s over the two shifts (the 90th percentile of dispatch time is over 64 s; needs 5 or more calls) Ask the supervisor for a second dispatcher.

If there is nothing to suggest, the card says "No suggestions right now."

What to do when...

Situation What to do

The row says No unit free Wait for an ambulance to clear. If one is out of service and ready again, click Back in service.
An ambulance breaks down Click Out of service on it. Its call is flagged Breakdown, and the reason is recorded for you.
A button you expect is missing Only the next valid button is shown. Check which card the call is in.

You pressed a button by mistake before the dialog Click Cancel in the dialog. The press is not recorded.
You want to practice Click Simulate a shift, or add calls and set Clock speed to 60x.
You want to start again Click Reset.
You want to see how the shift is going Open History.
A zone is red in History Hover over the red diamond to read why. Look at the Suggested actions next to it.
A zone has no free ambulance Look for T2 Rebalance in History.
You want to keep a record Click Download calls (CSV).
The map is blank Check your internet connection. The map loads from the internet.

Chapter 3Supervisor guide

This chapter is for the EMS operations supervisor. The supervisor view helps you answer four questions, in this order:

  1. Which zone needs attention?
  2. Which part of the call (dispatch, crew turnout or travel) is slow?
  3. What should we try?
  4. Did the fix work?

Click I'm a supervisor on the start page. You get two tabs: Charts and Actions. Charts answers questions 1 and 2. Actions answers questions 3 and 4.

Every card has one plain title. Under most cards, a More info toggle opens a short explanation of the card in two or three sentences. Use it whenever you want to know what you are looking at.

The Charts tab

The control bar

The control bar at the top of Charts sets what both tabs show.

Control Choices What it does
Chart Daily chart, Every call Which control chart to show (one at a time)
Zone Zone A to F, with its name Which zone the control chart shows. You can also click a zone on the map.
Priority 1: Life-threatening, 2: Urgent, 3: Non-urgent Which calls to look at. Each priority has its own target.
Dataset Demo, Baseline, Messy file Which data to look at (see chapter 1)
As of A date in the data The "today" for every card. Later calls are ignored. It moves to the last day of the data when you change the dataset.
Chart period Last 14, 28 or 42 days How much history every card uses. It also sets the control limits (see below).
Targets A button Opens the Targets dialog (see the end of this chapter)

Tip. To follow the demo story, choose the Demo data set and set As of to 2026-09-28. That is the day before the fix. Then set it to 2026-10-12 to see the result.

Control chart

The control chart is the main picture. It shows whether a zone is behaving the way it normally does. Choose Daily chart or Every call.

Daily chart

The Daily chart has two panels.

Top panel: "Zone X: daily average response."

  • Each dot is one day. Its height is that day's typical response time in minutes. ("Typical" is a geometric average. It is a fair average for times with a long slow tail.)
  • The center line is the zone's normal level. It is the average of the daily dots in the chart period.
  • The two dashed lines are the control limits. They are the upper control limit (UCL) and the lower control limit (LCL). They mark the normal range. They are the same for every day.
  • A red dot is a day that broke a flag rule (see below). Something probably changed.
  • A hollow ring is a "check the data" day: unusually fast. Check the records before you trust it.
  • The amber line is the target (the specification limit). It is a requirement from the standard. It is not part of the control limits.
  • A green vertical line marks a logged action (a fix).

Bottom panel: "Daily spread." It shows how spread out that day's calls were. A red dot means some calls that day were much slower than others.

What makes a dot red? One of these rules fired on the slow side:

Rule A red dot appears when...
R1 one day is far outside the normal range (beyond 3 sigma)
R2 2 of the last 3 days are well above normal (beyond 2 sigma)
R3 4 of the last 5 days are above normal (beyond 1 sigma)
R4 8 days in a row are above the center line
R5 6 days in a row are getting slower

A day on the fast side only says "check the data". It is not an alarm.

Important: limits come from the chart period. The control limits are worked out from the days in the chart period you selected. That is simple, and it keeps the chart, the map, the suggestions and the capability card all in agreement. But there is a trade-off. If a problem lasts for most of the period, it widens the limits and can hide itself. Example: in the demo data, as of Sep 28 with a 28-day period, Zone D's daily chart shows no red dots, because D's slowdown (from Sep 15) is inside the window. The other cards still show the problem: the Zone map is amber for D, Delay by stage blames crew turnout, and Capability says D is below the standard. Always read the daily chart together with those cards.

Every call

Every call shows each call, in the order the calls came in. The height is the response time in minutes. The scale is a log scale, so the slow tail does not squash the rest.

  • The center line is the typical call.
  • The dashed UCL and LCL are the normal range for a single call.
  • A red dot is a call above the UCL: an unusual delay worth looking at one by one.
  • The amber line is the target. Calls above it missed the target.

Statistics

The Statistics card sums up the chart period for all zones, for the priority you chose. The line under the title shows the dates and the number of calls.

Number What it means
Average The mean response time in minutes
Standard deviation How spread out the response times are. A big number means less predictable service.
On time The share of calls within the target. The goal is at least 90%.
Zones flagged How many zones are amber or red on the map, out of 6

Zone map

The Zone map shows Ithaca in six zones. The outlines are illustrative, not the official EMS districts. Each zone is colored by its status over the last 7 days:

Status Meaning
On track Nothing to worry about
Worth a look (amber) One warning sign, either a real change on the chart or a 90th percentile over the target
Act now (red) Both warning signs together
Not enough data Too few calls to judge
  • Hover over a zone to see its status and the reasons, such as "90th percentile 10.2 min vs. 9 min target" and "turnout causes 99% of minutes over target".
  • Click a zone to select it. The Zone menu and the control chart switch to that zone.
  • A zone that is slipping back after a logged fix is at least amber.
  • A single weak sign (for example, one rule fired once) does not turn a zone amber. It is mentioned in the zone's reasons: "one weak signal ... keep watching". This keeps false alarms rare.

Data quality

This card appears only when some calls were left out of the statistics. It lists each call and why: a missing time, times in the wrong order, a duplicate call ID, a zero response time, or an unknown zone or priority. These calls are never counted, so one bad record cannot distort a chart. The Messy file data set shows this card.

Delay by stage

A call has three stages: dispatch processing, crew turnout and travel. This card shows which stage causes the minutes over target in each zone. The line under the title shows the dates and the priority.

  • Each zone has one bar. The bar is split into three parts. Each part is that stage's share of the zone's minutes over target. The bar adds up to 100%.
  • The last bar, City, is all zones together.
  • Hover over a part of a bar to see its share, the total minutes over target, and the median time of that stage.

How to read it: the biggest part of a zone's bar is the stage to fix first. In the demo data, Zone D's bar is almost all crew turnout. Most other zones are mostly travel.

Zone comparison

This card answers "do the zones really differ?" in a sentence. For example: "Yes. Zone D is typically 6.0 min against 4.9 min in Zone A; a gap this big is very unlikely to be chance." If the gaps are small, it says "No clear difference: the gaps between zones could be chance."

Open More info for the test result (the F value and p-value) and a probability plot. See chapter 4 if you want the details.

Capability

The Capability card asks: "Can each zone meet the target, reliably?" It gives one plain verdict for each zone.

Verdict What it means
Very reliable 99 or more calls in 100 arrive within the target
Reliable 95 to 98 calls in 100 arrive within the target
Just meets the standard 90 to 94 in 100. There is little margin.
Below the standard Under 90 in 100
Can't judge yet The zone's times changed during the period, so a score would mislead. Fix the cause first.
Not enough calls Fewer than 30 calls in the period

Open More info for the Ppk and Cpk numbers behind the verdicts. The standard asks for at least 90 calls in 100 to be on time.

The Actions tab

Actions answers "what should we try, and did it work?" A line at the top reminds you of the data set, priority and date. Change them on the Charts tab.

Slip-back alerts

This card appears only when a zone is slipping back after a logged action. It names the zone and the warning sign. A zone that slips back is at least amber on the map.

Suggested actions

This card lists fixes ERGent suggests. Each one has:

  • a tag for the kind of fix: Crew readiness, Coverage, Routes, Dispatcher cover, Maintenance or Address checks;
  • a zone tag (or Citywide);
  • the suggestion, in plain words;
  • the evidence, so you can see why;
  • an estimate, such as "on time 78% → 97%". This is a best case: what the on-time share would be if that stage were as fast as the city's typical call.

Every suggestion is "for review by qualified staff". ERGent decides nothing. See chapter 4 for the rules behind S1 to S6.

To log a suggestion as an action:

Every number ERGent shows comes from one file of plain, tested functions (app/qc.py). The screens only draw those results. This chapter explains each statistic in plain words first, then gives the formula. Each one has four parts:

  • The formula
  • Watch out for

You do not need this chapter to use ERGent. It is here so you can explain every number.

Three ideas to know first

1. Times are in seconds inside the data and in minutes on screen. A response of 540 seconds is 9 minutes.

2. Control charts and capability use the logarithm of the time. Response times are skewed: most calls are quick, and a few are very slow. The log (written ln) pulls in that slow tail. After the math is done, ERGent turns the results back into minutes (with exp). So a "typical" time on a chart is a geometric average: exp(mean of ln minutes).

3. Control limits are not the target. A control limit (UCL and LCL) comes from the process itself. It says what is normal for this zone. A specification limit (the 9-minute target) comes from a standard. It says what is required. A zone can be inside its control limits and still miss the target, and the other way round. ERGent draws the target as a separate line, so you never confuse the two.

1. Stage times and data checks

What it tells you. How long each part of a call took, so you can say why a call was slow.

How to read it. A call has three stages. The prompt's metric, response, is turnout plus travel.

The formula.

processing = dispatched  - call_received      (dispatch: the call waits for an ambulance)
turnout    = en_route    - dispatched          (the crew gets moving)
travel     = on_scene    - en_route            (the drive)
response   = on_scene    - dispatched          (= turnout + travel)

A call is late when its response is greater than its priority's target. A stage is late when it is greater than that stage's target. The travel target is the response target minus the turnout target: for priority 1, 540 − 60 = 480 seconds.

Data checks. A call is left out of every statistic and listed, with its reason, if:

  1. its call ID is a duplicate (the repeat is left out; the first is kept);
  2. a needed time is missing (call received, dispatched, en route or on scene);
  3. a time is earlier than the one before it;
  4. its response time is zero;
  5. its zone or priority is unknown.

Watch out for. Processing happens before dispatch, so it sits outside the prompt's metric. ERGent still measures it, because a delay there also hurts the patient. Also, a crew might press "on scene" a little after arriving, so the times are only as good as the pressing habit.

2. Mean, standard deviation, percentiles and % on time

What it tells you. The mean is the average. The standard deviation (SD) says how spread out the times are. The median is the middle call. The 90th percentile is the time that 9 in 10 calls beat. The 95th percentile is the time that 19 in 20 calls beat. On time is the share of calls within the target.

How to read it. Look at the percentiles, not just the mean. The danger in emergency response is the slow tail, and the mean hides it. The standard says 90% of calls should be on time. That is the same as saying the 90th percentile should be at or under the target.

The formula.

mean        = (x1 + x2 + ... + xn) / n
SD          = sqrt( sum of (x - mean)^2 / (n - 1) )          (sample SD, n - 1)
on time (%) = 100 x (number of calls with x <= target) / n
percentile  : sort the values; position h = (n - 1) x p, counting from 0
              result = x[floor h] + (h - floor h) x ( x[floor h + 1] - x[floor h] )

This is the "type 7" percentile. It is the same as R's default and Excel's PERCENTILE.INC.

Worked example. Ten response times in minutes: 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 20, with a 9-minute target.

Number Value
Mean 9.2
Median 8.5
90th percentile 12.8 (h = 9 × 0.9 = 8.1, so 12 + 0.1 × (20 − 12))
95th percentile 16.4
On time 6 of 10 = 60%

The mean (9.2) looks close to the 9-minute target. But only 60% of the calls were on time, and one patient waited 20 minutes. That is why ERGent does not rely on the mean.

Watch out for. With one call, the percentiles equal that value and the SD cannot be worked out. With no calls, ERGent shows a dash.

3. Checking that logs behave (normality)

What it tells you. Whether the log of the response times is close to a bell curve. Several tests assume it is.

How to read it. Open More info on the Zone comparison card. A probability plot shows the points. If they sit close to a straight line, the log times look normal. The Shapiro-Wilk test gives a number p. A p above 0.05 means "no sign of a problem".

The formula. The Shapiro-Wilk W statistic and its p-value (from the SciPy library), on ln(minutes).

Watch out for. With hundreds of calls, even a tiny bend can give a small p. Judge by the plot as well. ERGent needs at least 3 values that are not all the same.

4. The daily X̄-S chart

What it tells you. Whether a zone is behaving the way it normally does. Each dot is one day. A red dot is a day when the zone was slower than its own normal range.

How to read it. The top panel (X̄) shows each day's typical response time. The center line is the normal level. The dashed lines are the UCL and LCL. The bottom panel (S) shows how spread out the day's calls were. A red dot means something changed. Open Delay by stage to see which stage.

The formula. The subgroup is one zone-day. Work on ln(minutes):

daily point     = mean of ln(minutes) for that day's calls
center          = mean of the daily points in the chart period
sigma_xbar      = standard deviation of the daily points in the chart period (n - 1)
UCL, LCL        = center +/- 3 x sigma_xbar         (the same for every day)

S chart:
s_bar           = mean of the daily standard deviations (days with 2 or more calls)
n_bar           = average number of calls per day in the period (rounded, at least 2)
c4(n)           = sqrt( 2 / (n - 1) ) x Gamma(n / 2) / Gamma( (n - 1) / 2 )
B4 = 1 + 3 x sqrt(1 - c4^2) / c4        B3 = 1 - 3 x sqrt(1 - c4^2) / c4   (never below 0)
S limits        = B4 x s_bar (upper) and B3 x s_bar (lower)

Here are some values of the constants (they match the standard tables):

Calls per day (n̄) c4 B3 B4
2 0.7979 0 3.267
3 0.8862 0 2.568
5 0.9400 0 2.089
7 0.9594 0.118 1.882
10 0.9727 0.284 1.716

The limits are then shown back in minutes (exp). The chart is judged with the flag rules in section 6.

Watch out for: the chart period sets the limits. In ERGent, the limits come from the chart period you select (14, 28 or 42 days). The team chose this on purpose. It is simple, and it keeps the chart, the zone map, the suggestions and the capability card in agreement. But there is a cost. If a problem lasts for most of the period, it widens the limits and can hide itself.

For example, in the demo data, Zone D's crew turnout got slow on Sep 15. As of Sep 28 with a 28-day period, the period includes both normal days and slow days. The limits stretch to about 3.3 to 10.5 minutes, and no day breaks them. Zone D's chart shows no red dots. If the limits were taken from the first 14 days instead (before the problem), the same chart would flag Zone D on 11 days.

So: read the daily chart together with the Zone map, Delay by stage and Capability. In this example, the Zone map is amber for D (its 90th percentile is 10.2 min against a 9-min target), Delay by stage says 92% of its overrun is crew turnout, and Capability says D is below the standard.

Also watch out for days with very few calls. Their dots are noisy, and a one-call day has no spread, so it has no point on the S chart.

5. The every-call chart (individuals chart)

What it tells you. Whether a single call was unusually slow. It looks at each call, in the order the calls came in.

How to read it. Each dot is one call. The dashed lines (UCL and LCL) are the normal range for a single call. A red dot above the UCL is an unusual delay worth checking one by one: a breakdown, a wrong address, a closed road. The amber line is the target. The scale is a log scale.

The formula. This is the course's I-MR (individuals and moving range) method, on ln(minutes):

moving range   MR_i = abs( x_i - x_(i-1) )            (jump from one call to the next)
MR_bar         = mean of the moving ranges
center         = mean of ln(minutes)
sigma          = MR_bar / 1.128                         (1.128 is the constant d2 for pairs)
UCL, LCL       = center +/- 3 x sigma
MR upper limit = 3.267 x MR_bar                         (computed, not drawn)

Worked example. Five values 10, 12, 11, 15, 12. The moving ranges are 2, 1, 4, 3, so MR̄ = 2.5. The center is 12 and σ = 2.5 / 1.128 = 2.216. The limits are 5.351 and 18.649.

Watch out for. It needs at least 2 calls. If every call is the same, there is no spread, so there are no limits ("no variation"). The calls are assumed to be roughly independent, which is stated but not tested. As on the daily chart, the limits come from the calls in the chart period.

6. The flag rules, and what makes a zone amber or red

What it tells you. The rules decide which dots on the daily chart turn red. The stricter zone health rule decides whether a zone on the map is on track, worth a look, or act now.

How to read it. A dot above the center line is on the slow side. That is an alarm. A dot below the center line is on the fast side. That only says "check the data".

The formula. Let z = (daily point - center) / sigma_xbar. So "1 sigma" means one sigma_xbar. The rules are:

Rule A flag fires at a day when...
R1 that day's point is beyond 3 sigma
R2 2 of the last 3 points are beyond 2 sigma, on the same side
R3 4 of the last 5 points are beyond 1 sigma, on the same side
R4 8 points in a row are on the same side of the center line
R5 6 points in a row are steadily rising (or falling)

R1 to R4 are the Western Electric rules. R5 is a trend rule. Together they catch both sudden jumps and slow drifts.

Zone health. On the map, each zone looks at the last 7 days. First, ERGent decides if there is a chart signal:

chart signal = rule R1 fired in the last 7 days,
               OR 2 or more DIFFERENT slow-side rules fired in the last 7 days

red    ("Act now")       = chart signal AND the 90th percentile of the last 7 days is over the target
amber  ("Worth a look")  = exactly one of those two
green  ("On track")      = neither
unknown                  = not enough data to set limits

A zone that is slipping back after a logged fix (section 18) is at least amber.

Why stricter? One weak rule on its own is drawn on the chart but does not count. It appears in the zone's reasons as "one weak signal ... keep watching". The team tested this. On pure-noise simulations, a stable zone-week shows amber by chance about 2.5% of the time with this rule, against about 7.7% if any single rule counted.

Watch out for. Even so, some false alarms are expected. Amber means "worth a look", not "something is broken". Red means act. A zone can also be amber only because its 90th percentile is just over the target, with no change on the chart.

7. Zones as subgroups (the dispatcher's History chart)

What it tells you. Which zones are slower than the city as a whole, in the current and previous shift.

How to read it. Each zone is drawn as a box plot (the box holds the middle half of its calls) with a diamond for its average. The grey line is the city's center. The short dashed lines are that zone's limits. A red diamond is a zone beyond its upper limit: slower than the city. A hollow ring is a zone beyond its lower limit: faster, so check the data. Hover over a red diamond to read why.

The formula. Each zone is one subgroup of ln(minutes) (calls that reached the scene, for the priority you chose):

center      = mean of the six zone averages
sigma_short = sqrt( mean of the zone variances )         (zones with 2 or more calls)
limits      = center +/- 3 x sigma_short / sqrt(n_zone)  (wider for zones with few calls)
z           = (zone average - center) / ( sigma_short / sqrt(n_zone) )
flag        = "above" if z > 3, "below" if z < -3

Zones have no time order, so only the "beyond the limit" rule is used. This is like an analysis of means.

The hover text for a red zone says: its typical time against the city's, the stage causing the most minutes over target, and the most common delay reason among its late calls.

Watch out for. A zone with few calls has wide limits, so it is hard to flag. A zone with no calls shows nothing. The two shifts together can be a small sample early in a shift.

8. Delay by stage

What it tells you. Which stage (dispatch, crew turnout or travel) causes the minutes over target.

How to read it. For each zone, the three parts of the bar add up to 100%. The biggest part is the stage to fix first. Hover over a part to see its share, the minutes over target, and the stage's median time.

The formula.

stage excess = max( 0, stage time - stage target )         for each call, in minutes
share        = a stage's total excess / the sum of the three stages' total excess

Watch out for. A share is only about minutes over target. A stage that stays under target has no share, even if it is slow compared with other stages. If nothing is over target, all shares are 0.

9. Capability

What it tells you. Whether a zone can meet the target reliably, not just on average.

How to read it. ERGent turns the result into a plain verdict.

Verdict Expected calls on time (rounded)
Very reliable 99 in 100 or more
Reliable 95 to 98 in 100
Just meets the standard 90 to 94 in 100
Below the standard under 90 in 100
Can't judge yet The zone is not stable (see below)
Not enough calls Fewer than 30 calls

The formula. The target is an upper limit only (a response cannot be "too fast"), so ERGent uses the upper side of the capability indices, on ln(minutes):

USL        = ln( target in minutes )
Ppk        = ( USL - mean of ln x ) / ( 3 x SD of ln x )              uses the overall spread (sigma_total)
Cpk        = ( USL - mean of ln x ) / ( 3 x sigma_short )              uses the spread within each day
sigma_short = sqrt( mean of the daily variances )
expected on time (%) = 100 x Phi( 3 x Ppk )                            Phi = the standard normal curve

Some handy values:

Ppk Expected on time
0.33 83.9%
0.43 90.1% (the standard's 90% line)
0.50 93.3%
0.67 97.8%
0.80 99.2%
1.00 99.9%

The verdict is judged on the rounded percentage, so the label always agrees with the number in the text.

Stable only. A capability score only makes sense for a stable zone. ERGent shows a score only when the zone's daily chart has no slow-side flag in the chart period and the zone has at least 30 calls. Otherwise it says "Can't judge yet" or "Not enough calls".

Watch out for. Cpk and Ppk differ when a zone drifts between days. Ppk includes the drift; Cpk does not. The numbers sit behind More info. The score assumes the log times are roughly normal. Because the daily chart's limits come from the chart period, a long problem can hide on the chart (section 4). In that case capability may still say "Below the standard", as it does for Zone D in the demo.

10. Zone comparison (ANOVA)

What it tells you. Whether the zones really differ, or whether the gaps could be chance.

How to read it. ERGent answers in a sentence: "Yes. Zone D is typically 6.0 min against 4.9 min in Zone A ..." or "No clear difference ...". The typical times are geometric averages.

The formula. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on ln(minutes) of each zone's calls:

SSB = sum over zones of  n_zone x ( zone mean - grand mean )^2      (between zones)
SSW = sum over zones of  sum of ( x - zone mean )^2                   (within zones)
F   = ( SSB / (k - 1) ) / ( SSW / (N - k) )                           k zones, N calls
p   = the chance of an F this big if the zones were really the same  (from the F distribution)

ERGent says "Yes" when p < 0.05.

Worked example. Zone A = [1, 2, 3] and zone B = [4, 5, 6]. SSB = 13.5 and SSW = 4, so F = 13.5 / (4 / 4) = 13.5 with 1 and 4 degrees of freedom.

Watch out for. A zone with fewer than 2 calls is left out. If every call in each zone is identical, the test cannot run. ANOVA says that zones differ, not which one. Look at the typical times for that. A "Yes" with hundreds of calls can come from a small gap, so check that the gap is large enough to matter.

11. Welch's t-test and the before/after check

What it tells you. Whether response times really dropped after an action.

How to read it. The Result card shows a verdict: Improved, Got worse, No clear change yet or Not enough data.

The formula. ERGent compares the 14 days before the action date with the days from the action date on (up to 14), using Welch's one-sided t-test on ln(minutes). Welch's test does not assume both groups have the same spread.

t  = ( mean_before - mean_after ) / sqrt( s_before^2 / n_before + s_after^2 / n_after )
df = Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
p  = chance of a t this big if nothing changed (one-sided: "after is lower")

Improved            if p < 0.05
Got worse           if p > 0.95     (it got slower, and too much to be chance)
No clear change yet otherwise
Not enough data     if either side has fewer than 2 calls, or there is no variation at all

Worked example. Before = [10, 12, 14] and after = [6, 8, 10]. The means are 12 and 8. Each variance is 4, so t = 4 / √(4/3 + 4/3) = 2.449 with 4 degrees of freedom.

Steady. ERGent also checks whether the new level is steady. It draws the zone's daily chart after the action, with limits from the first 14 days after the action, and looks for a slow-side flag.

Watch out for. A better time after a fix does not prove the fix caused it. Other things may have changed. The check shows an apparent change. Staff judge the cause. If the after period is 14 days or shorter, the "steady" check judges those days against limits made from the same days.

12. Impact estimate

What it tells you. A best-case guess of how much better on-time performance could be if one stage were fixed. For example "on time 78% → 97%".

How to read it. It answers: "if this zone's slow stage were as fast as the city's typical call, how many more calls would be on time?" It is an estimate, not a promise.

The formula. Take the flagged stage (turnout or travel). Cap each call's stage time at the city's median for that stage (priority and last 14 days). Then recount.

new response = response - stage time + min( stage time, cap )
on time      = new response <= the call's response target

For the dispatch stage, "on time" means dispatch time at or under the processing target (64 s by default).

Worked example. Turnout [3, 1, 4, 1] minutes and travel [7, 7, 6, 9] minutes, target 9, city median turnout 1 minute. Responses are 10, 8, 10, 10, so 1 of 4 is on time (25%). With turnout capped at 1 minute they become 8, 8, 7, 10, so 3 of 4 are on time (75%).

Watch out for. Real results are usually smaller. A fix rarely brings every call down to the median.

13. Suggested actions for the supervisor (rules S1 to S6)

What they tell you. Fixed "if this, then suggest that" rules turn the numbers into something to try. Each suggestion shows its evidence and says "for review by qualified staff".

How to read them. The rules look at the last 14 days up to the as-of date. A stage's share is its excess divided by the zone's total excess (section 8). Rules S1 to S3 only apply to zones that are amber or red.

Rule Tag If Then suggest
S1 Crew readiness Turnout share is 50% or more Review crew readiness at that zone's station. It names a shift when that shift holds 40% or more of the turnout excess and at least 1.5 times its share of the calls.
S2 Coverage Travel share is 50% or more, 30% or more of late calls are coded "unit from another zone", and the zone borrows ambulances at least 1.5 times the city rate Consider posting an extra ambulance in that zone during the busiest three hours of late calls
S3 Routes Travel share is 50% or more, and "traffic or road closure" is the top code (at least 3 calls and at least 30% of late calls) Check road works and alternative routes. It says "weekdays" if 80% or more of late calls fall on weekdays.
S4 Dispatcher cover Citywide, all priorities: in an hour of the day with above-median call counts, the 90th percentile of dispatch time is over the processing target Add dispatcher cover in those hours
S5 Maintenance One ambulance has 2 or more breakdowns in the window Schedule preventive maintenance for that ambulance
S6 Address checks A zone has at least 5 late calls, and 20% or more of them have "unclear address" Use an address-verification script

The formula for the evidence and the estimate is in sections 8 and 12.

Watch out for. The rules only see what the crews record. If delay reasons are missing, S2, S3, S5 and S6 cannot fire. The tool decides nothing. A qualified person decides.

14. Dispatcher tips (rules T1 to T6)

What they tell you. Live suggestions for the dispatcher, from the board right now and the last two shifts.

How to read them. They appear in Suggested actions on the History tab.

15. Slip-back

What it tells you. Whether a zone is drifting back to its old problem after a fix.

How to read it. A Slip-back alert names the zone and the warning sign, and the zone is at least amber on the map.

The formula. For each logged action, ERGent builds the zone's daily chart with limits from the first 7 days after the action. If a slow-side flag appears on a day after those 7 days, it raises an alert.

Watch out for. It needs enough days after the action. In the demo data, there is no slip-back for Zone D, which is the right answer.

Chapter 5Glossary and FAQ

Glossary

Terms are in alphabetical order.

Term Meaning
90th percentile The time that 9 in 10 calls beat. The slowest 1 in 10 calls take longer. The standard asks for it to be at or under the target.
Action A fix that the supervisor logs, with a date, a zone, a stage, what was done and an owner. ERGent then checks whether it worked.
Alarm A flag on the slow side of a control chart. A flag on the fast side only says "check the data".
ANOVA A test that asks whether several groups (here, zones) really differ, or whether the gaps could be chance.
Average (mean) The sum of the times divided by how many there are. It hides slow calls, so ERGent also shows percentiles.
Baseline A stretch of normal days used to set a zone's normal range. In ERGent, the chart period plays this role.
CAD Computer-aided dispatch. The city's system that keeps one row per 911 call and stamps the times.
Capability (Ppk, Cpk) A measure of whether a stable process can meet its target reliably. ERGent shows it as a plain verdict.
Center line The middle line of a control chart: the zone's normal level.
Chart period How many days of history the cards use (14, 28 or 42). It also sets the control limits.
Chart signal A strong warning on the daily chart: rule R1 fired, or two or more different slow-side rules fired in the last 7 days.
Clock speed How fast the board clock runs when you log calls by hand (1x, 10x, 30x or 60x).
Common cause Ordinary, everyday variation. The ups and downs inside the control limits.
Control chart A chart of a process over time, with a center line and control limits. It shows when something unusual happens.
Control limits (UCL and LCL) The upper and lower lines on a control chart, at 3 sigma from the center. They show what is normal for the process. They are not the target.
Crew turnout The time from dispatch until the crew leaves (dispatched to en_route). Target: 60 seconds.
Delay reason One of six codes the crew picks when a stage runs late: traffic or road closure, ambulance breakdown, unit from another zone, unclear address, weather, crew not ready.
Dispatch processing The time from the call arriving until an ambulance is dispatched (call_received to dispatched). Target: 64 seconds.
False alarm A flag that fires by chance when nothing has really changed. A few are expected.
Geometric mean The "typical" value for skewed data: exp of the mean of the logs. ERGent uses it for a typical response time.
Log scale (ln) A way of measuring that pulls in a long slow tail. ERGent's charts and tests use the natural log of the time.
Median The middle value: half the calls are faster, half are slower.
Moving range (MR) The size of the jump from one call to the next.
NFPA 1710 A US standard for fire and EMS response. It is the source of the priority 1 targets (64 s, 60 s and 480 s).
On time A call whose response was at or under its target. The goal is at least 90% of calls.
p-value The chance of seeing a difference this big if nothing had really changed. A small p (under 0.05) means the difference is probably real.
Percentile The value that a given share of the data falls under. The 90th percentile is beaten by 90% of calls.
Poka-yoke Mistake-proofing. The screen makes the wrong entry hard to make, for example by showing only the next valid button.
Priority The urgency code for a call: 1 life-threatening, 2 urgent, 3 non-urgent. Each has its own response target.
Response time The time from dispatch to arrival on scene (dispatched to on_scene). It is turnout plus travel. This is the prompt's metric.
Shift An eight-hour block: day 06:00 to 14:00, evening 14:00 to 22:00, night 22:00 to 06:00.
Sigma (σ) A measure of spread, like a standard deviation. Control limits sit 3 sigma from the center.
Slip-back A zone drifting back to its old problem after a fix.
Special cause Something that changed the process: a breakdown, road works, a new crew habit. A flag points to one.
Specification limit (spec limit) The requirement, such as the 9-minute target. It comes from a standard, not from the process.
Stable A process that stays inside its control limits with no flags. Capability only makes sense for a stable process.
Standard deviation (SD) How spread out the values are around their mean. A big SD means less predictable service.
Subgroup A small group of values charted as one point. In the daily chart, a subgroup is one zone-day. In the History chart, it is one zone.
Suggested action A fixed "if this, then suggest that" rule on the data, shown with its evidence.
Synthetic data Invented data, made by a script. ERGent contains no real patient data.
Travel time The drive to the scene (en_route to on_scene).
Welch's t-test A test of whether two groups have different averages that does not assume equal spread. ERGent uses it for before and after.
X̄ chart (x-bar chart) A control chart of group averages. ERGent's daily chart shows each day's average.
Zone One of six areas of Ithaca (A to F). The outlines on the map are illustrative, not official EMS districts.

Frequently asked questions

1. Why not just use the monthly average?

An average blends fast and slow calls. A handful of very slow calls can hide inside a good-looking average. Take ten calls with times 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 20 minutes. The average is 9.2 minutes, close to a 9-minute target. But only 6 of the 10 were on time, and one patient waited 20 minutes. ERGent shows the 90th and 95th percentiles and on-time share next to the average, and splits each zone and each day. That is where the slow calls show up.

2. Why does ERGent use logs?

Response times are skewed. Most are quick, and a few are very slow. The log pulls in the slow tail, so control charts and tests behave. ERGent turns the results back into minutes before showing them, so you never have to think in logs.

3. What is the difference between amber and red?

On the Zone map, amber ("Worth a look") means one warning sign: either the daily chart shows a strong change, or the zone's 90th percentile is over its target. Red ("Act now") means both together. Amber is worth checking. Red needs action. Some amber zones are false alarms, and the stricter amber rule makes them rare.

4. Why does Zone D's daily chart show no red dots when the Zone map is amber?

The control limits come from the chart period you picked. If a problem lasts for most of that period, it widens the limits and can hide itself on the daily chart. That is a trade-off the team chose for simplicity and consistency. The Zone map, Delay by stage and Capability cards do not hide it. Read the daily chart together with them.

5. What does "Can't judge yet" mean on the Capability card?

The zone's times changed during the period (it has a flag on its daily chart). A reliability score for a zone that is changing would mislead. Find and fix the cause first, then judge again.

6. What does "Not enough calls" mean?

A zone needs at least 30 calls in the chart period for a trustworthy score. With fewer calls, the numbers are too uncertain.

7. Is the data real?

No. All the data is synthetic. A script made it with a fixed random seed. It has no real patients, addresses or names. It is shaped like real field data so that the tool has something realistic to analyze. The Ithaca zone outlines are illustrative too.

8. Where do the targets come from?

For priority 1, from NFPA 1710, a US standard: 64 seconds to dispatch, 60 seconds for the crew to get moving and 8 minutes to travel, so 9 minutes from dispatch to arrival, each met by at least 90% of calls. The priority 2 (15 minutes) and priority 3 (20 minutes) targets are our own assumptions, because the standard does not set them. A supervisor can change every target with the Targets button.

9. Does ERGent dispatch ambulances or change staffing?

No. ERGent flags and suggests. Every suggestion is "for review by qualified staff", and the dispatcher's tips are "for the dispatcher's judgment". People make every decision.

10. What is the difference between a control limit and the target?

A control limit (UCL and LCL) comes from the process: it shows what is normal for a zone. The target (a specification limit) comes from a standard: it shows what is required. A zone can be within its normal range and still miss the target, or beat the target and still be changing. ERGent draws them as different lines so you never confuse them.
